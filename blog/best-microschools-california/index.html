Khan Lab School — Mountain View

Khan Lab School is a mastery-based independent school in Silicon Valley serving transitional kindergarten through grade 12. A microschool-style approach lets students move at their own pace: roughly half of each day goes to collaborative, real-world projects and the other half to building fluency in core skills. Accredited by WASC, the school reports students advancing 1.5 to 2 grade levels per year.

Two campuses house the program — a lower school in Palo Alto for TK-5 and a middle and upper school in Mountain View for grades 6-12. Teacher-guides facilitate the learning rather than lecture, and high-school students can take on college-level coursework early.

Khan Lab School grew out of the Khan Academy founder's experiment in personalized, competency-based learning, and a microschool structure is central to how it operates: small groups, mixed-pace progression, and mastery before moving on. See the Khan Lab School listing for contact details and to start a tour.

Praxis Elite Sports Academy Junior Prep — San Diego County

Praxis Elite Sports Academy Junior Prep is an athlete-focused microschool in San Diego that serves 7th and 8th grade. Each day runs two academic periods and two athletic periods, so student-athletes train and study in equal measure. A partner in the KaiPod Learning microschool network, Praxis offers both full-time and part-time enrollment and runs an 8th-plus reclassification year for students preparing for high school.

Dedicated classrooms sit alongside an in-house weight-training and turf facility, which is what lets the school build athletics into the regular school day rather than bolting it on after hours.

This is a narrow, specific fit: families with a committed middle-school athlete who want academics and serious training under one roof. Families outside that niche will find the model heavy on court and field time. Check the Praxis Elite Sports Academy listing for schedule and enrollment details.

The Neos Bayview Academy — San Francisco

The Neos Bayview Academy is a learner-driven K-5 microschool in San Francisco's Bayview neighborhood. Character traits such as focus and resilience anchor the program alongside core literacy and math instruction, and adaptive technology lets each student work toward mastery at their own level. Tuition is $9,050 per year, with scholarships offered and Bayview residents given priority.

The Neos Bayview Academy follows an Acton Academy-style model with a Christ-centered mission and a small, community-oriented setting at 950 Gilman Ave. School tours run every Monday.

At $9,050 a year, The Neos Bayview Academy sits below the roughly $10,000 typical full-time microschool tuition and well under San Francisco private-school norms, and the scholarship priority for Bayview families is a deliberate access choice. Read the Neos Bayview Academy listing for scholarship and enrollment information.

How much do microschools cost in California?

Among California microschools that publish tuition in our directory, prices start around $9,050 per year, and a typical full-time microschool nationally runs near $10,000. Many California microschools do not post a price publicly, so confirm tuition directly with each school. Part-time and a-few-days-a-week options usually cost much less than full-time enrollment.

California is different from school-choice states like Arizona and Florida on funding. As of this post, California has no statewide Education Savings Account or private-school voucher program — a measure to create one is headed to the November 2026 ballot but is not law. Families generally pay California microschool tuition out of pocket today. Official, current information on schooling options and private-school rules in the state lives at the California Department of Education.

One funding path does exist for some programs: certain California microschools partner with charter schools and accept charter enrichment or independent-study funds toward tuition or activity fees. Wild Hearts Adventure Co in Visalia, for example, notes it accepts charter-school enrichment funding. Ask each school whether it works with a charter and what that covers before you assume a price.

How to choose a microschool in California

To choose a microschool in California, start with your child's needs and your commute, then tour two or three schools in person. Watch how guides interact with students, ask for a typical daily schedule hour by hour, and confirm group size, tuition, and what funding the school accepts. Fit matters more than reputation, and a good microschool will answer every question directly.

A practical order of operations:

  1. Shortlist by model and location. Decide whether a mastery-based, learner-driven, athlete-focused, or another model fits your child, then filter microschools in California by city.
  2. Learn the vocabulary. Terms like learner-driven, mastery-based, and hybrid each shape the school day. Our glossary of terms defines them plainly.
  3. Tour and ask hard questions. Group size and ratio, the lead guide's background, how the school handles a struggling student, safety, and records or transcripts.
  4. Confirm the money. Get current tuition in writing, ask about scholarships, and ask whether the school partners with a charter for any funding.

Frequently asked questions

How many microschools are in California?

microschool.directory lists 29 microschools and homeschool co-ops across California, in about 26 cities. San Francisco has the most listed so far. The directory adds new schools regularly, so the live count keeps growing.

How much do microschools cost in California?

Among California schools that publish tuition in our directory, prices start near $9,050 per year, and a typical full-time microschool nationally runs around $10,000. Many California schools do not post a price, and part-time options cost less. Confirm current tuition with each school directly.

Does California have an ESA or scholarship for microschool tuition?

Not statewide as of mid-2026. California has no Education Savings Account or private-school voucher program, though a ballot measure to create one goes to voters in November 2026. Some microschools partner with charter schools and accept charter enrichment funds, so ask each school what funding it works with.

What should I look for when choosing a California microschool?

Shortlist by learning model and location, then tour two or three schools in person. Ask for an hour-by-hour daily schedule, group size and adult-to-student ratio, tuition in writing, and what funding the school accepts. Fit with your child matters more than reputation.

Are microschools in California legit?

Yes. Microschools are legal in California and generally operate under the state's existing private-school or homeschool rules rather than as chartered public schools. Accreditation is optional, so ask each school whether it is accredited and how it handles records and transcripts. The California Department of Education is the authoritative source on the rules.
